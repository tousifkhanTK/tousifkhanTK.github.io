Editing for the Future: What Video Means in the Age of AI and Automation

Tousif Khan · 3 min read

Last month, I had the chance to join a panel on digital media storytelling, where the conversation kept circling back to one central question: What does the future of video editing look like when automation, machine learning, and AI tools are changing the way content is created?

From TikTok auto-edit features to AI-powered color grading and even generative b-roll, editing no longer starts with a blank timeline. And while this may sound like a threat to creativity, many industry leaders argue the opposite: video editors are now positioned to do more meaningful work than ever before.

What does automation mean for video editors?

It’s difficult to find a production team today that hasn’t automated at least one part of their editing workflow. From transcriptions to rough cuts, tools like Descript, Adobe Premiere’s AI features, and Runway are speeding up time-consuming, repeatable tasks.

But what does this mean for the role of the human editor?

During his keynote, filmmaker and technologist Alex Lindsay posed the question: “If machines can handle the assembly, what is left for us?” His answer: storytelling.

Machines can cut clips together, but they don’t understand tension, emotion, or nuance. They can analyze data, but they don’t feel what an audience feels in the final frame. For editors, this is the upside: by letting automation handle the heavy lifting, we can focus on pacing, tone, and meaning — the essence of what makes a story resonate.

The misconception, however, is that automation replaces creativity. As Alex reminded us, “A tool doesn’t make an edit. An editor makes a choice.”

How do we edit for an unknown future?

Storytelling is timeless, but the formats keep changing. Ten years ago, editors worked primarily in film, TV, and web video. Today, short-form dominates social platforms, livestreams demand real-time editing, and immersive media like VR is gaining ground.

So how do editors prepare for a future that feels uncertain?

Video educator Kelsey Brannan (known as Premiere Gal) shared a useful perspective: “Every time the medium changes, the principles stay the same. Good editing is invisible, and good storytelling is universal.”

Just as UX designers once fought for their place at the strategy table, video editors today are entering a new stage: no longer just “button-pushers,” but storytellers, collaborators, and problem-solvers who shape how stories are experienced.

In other words, the job is expanding — not shrinking.

Are we editing for audiences or ourselves?

Another thread that came up during the panel was whether we, as editors, are always in tune with what our audiences actually want.

Research shows viewers say they love options — hundreds of shows, millions of videos — but in reality, they often choose the simplest, most accessible format. It’s the same paradox that UX psychologists like Joe Leech point out: people think they want more, but too much choice leads to decision fatigue.

For video editors, this raises a vital question: are we cutting content for ourselves, or for the real behaviors of our viewers?

Data gives us clues (watch time, drop-off points, click-through rates), but editing is still about instinct. An editor who understands human psychology — attention span, emotional triggers, storytelling arcs — will always outshine one who simply follows the metrics.

As one editor on the panel put it: “The algorithm can tell you what people watch. But only an editor can tell you why.”

The future of video editing

If AI can generate clips and automation can cut timelines, where does this leave editors? The answer is clear: at the heart of storytelling.

Automation doesn’t remove the role of the editor — it sharpens it. It gives us more time to focus on emotion, creativity, and impact. It challenges us to think beyond the timeline, to design not just for the present formats, but for the evolving ways audiences experience stories.

Just as design has its “UX tipping point,” video editing is entering its own — where the craft is less about assembling content and more about shaping meaning. And that, ultimately, is what makes editing such a uniquely human art.
